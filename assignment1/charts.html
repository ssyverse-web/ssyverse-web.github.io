Assignment 1: Human Genetic Data

Data Visualization Portfolio · DSC 502

This project explores patterns of genetic variation using principal component analysis (PCA) and interactive Vega-Lite visualizations.

Dataset

This project uses the Human Genetic Data dataset available from Kaggle. The version used here, genetic_data_train.csv, contains 183 observations and 11 columns: ten precomputed principal-component scores (PC1–PC10) and a categorical Ancestry variable.

Five ancestry categories are represented in the data: African, EastAsian, European, NativeAmerican, and Oceanian. This portfolio focuses primarily on PC1 and PC2, with PC3 introduced in the third visualization. The ancestry variable is used as a categorical label for comparing how observations are distributed in principal-component space.

Source: Kaggle — Human Genetic Data

Methodology: Principal Component Analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) is a dimensionality reduction technique used to summarize variation across many variables using a smaller number of new variables called principal components. Mathematically, PCA identifies orthogonal directions in the data that successively account for as much variation as possible. PC1 is the direction explaining the greatest amount of variation, PC2 explains the greatest remaining variation subject to being orthogonal to PC1, and subsequent components continue this process.

In genetic applications, the original variables may consist of measurements at many genetic markers, such as single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). PCA transforms this high-dimensional genetic information into coordinates for each individual on the principal-component axes. Individuals with similar genetic profiles tend to have similar coordinates and therefore appear close together when plotted in principal-component space. PCA can consequently be useful for exploratory visualization of population structure and patterns of genetic similarity.

An important qualification is that the principal components themselves do not inherently represent particular ancestries or biological processes. Their interpretation depends on the genetic variables, individuals, preprocessing, and sampling scheme used to calculate them. Other factors, including relatedness, linkage disequilibrium, uneven sampling, and technical artifacts, can also influence PCA patterns.

For this project, I use the principal-component scores supplied in the Kaggle dataset rather than recomputing PCA from an underlying genotype matrix. The visualizations therefore examine the distribution of the provided PC scores and their relationship to the dataset's ancestry labels.

Visualization 1

PC1 vs. PC2

This scatterplot provides a baseline view of the first two principal components. Each point represents one observation, with PC1 mapped to the x-axis and PC2 mapped to the y-axis. The concentration of observations into several regions of principal-component space indicates that the dataset contains substantial structure in the genetic variation summarized by these two axes. At this stage, however, the plot does not tell us what those clusters correspond to biologically; it simply reveals the underlying structure without using the ancestry labels.

Visualization 2

PC1 vs. PC2 with Ancestry

This visualization retains the PC1 and PC2 axes but adds Ancestry as a categorical color encoding. The ancestry labels make it apparent that much of the clustering visible in the first plot corresponds to differences among the ancestry categories represented in the dataset. In genetic terms, this illustrates how variation summarized by the principal components can be associated with population structure. Because ancestry is supplied as a label in the dataset, however, the PCA itself should not be interpreted as having independently discovered these categories. Instead, the plot allows the genetic structure revealed by PCA to be compared with the labels attached to the observations.

Visualization 3

PC1 vs. PC2 with Ancestry and PC3 Magnitude

This visualization retains the PC1–PC2 representation and the ancestry color encoding while adding the magnitude of PC3 as point size. PC3 represents a third axis of variation that is not visible in the first two-dimensional projection, allowing an additional quantitative dimension to be shown simultaneously. Larger circles represent observations with greater absolute PC3 values, while the tooltip provides the original signed PC3 value. The additional encoding gives a richer view of the supplied PCA results, although the larger symbols can overlap and make individual observations somewhat more difficult to distinguish.

Visualization 4

PC1 vs. PC2 by Ancestry

This visualization separates the observations into ancestry-specific panels while retaining PC1 and PC2 as the horizontal and vertical axes. Unlike the color-based representation in Visualization 2, this design reduces visual overlap between ancestry groups and makes the distribution within each group easier to examine. The panels use identical PC1 and PC2 scales so that the positions, spread, and potential outlying observations of the groups can be compared directly.

Interpretation and Limitations

These visualizations show patterns in the principal-component scores supplied by the dataset and their relationship to the provided ancestry labels. They are useful for exploratory visualization of genetic structure, but PCA is a descriptive dimension-reduction method rather than, by itself, a complete model of population history or ancestry.

Different choices of genetic markers, preprocessing, sampling, and PCA methodology can affect the resulting axes and patterns. In addition, the present dataset provides PC scores rather than the underlying genotype matrix, and information about the original PCA procedure is limited. The figures should therefore be interpreted as visualizations of the supplied PCA results rather than as an independently reproduced population-genetic analysis.
