Portfolio 3: When Accurate Data Mislead

Data Visualization Portfolio · DSC 502

This project explores Simpson's paradox through historical data on graduate admissions at the University of California, Berkeley. It demonstrates how aggregating accurate statistics can encourage a different conclusion from examining the same data within relevant subgroups.

Dataset and Background

This analysis uses the University of California, Berkeley's graduate admissions data for 1973. The data record whether applicants were admitted or rejected, their sex, and the department to which they applied. The analysis focuses on departments A through F, excluding the "Other" category. Both figures use these same six departments and the same underlying application counts.

Data source: Berkeley admissions CSV · UCBAdmissions documentation

Simpson's paradox describes situations in which a relationship observed in aggregated data differs from the relationship observed within subgroups. Here, the overall admission rate is higher for male applicants, while female applicants have higher admission rates in four of the six departments. Differences in how applicants are distributed among departments help explain this contrast.

Figure 1: Overall Admission Rates

Intentionally misleading: department-level context omitted

INTENTIONALLY MISLEADING FIGURE. The aggregate admission rates shown here are calculated correctly. The misleading aspect is the omission of department-level context, which can encourage readers to draw an overly broad conclusion from the overall difference.

This bar chart combines applicants from departments A through F and compares the overall admission rates for male and female applicants. Approximately 44.5% of male applicants were admitted, compared with 30.4% of female applicants. Viewed alone, the chart may suggest that male applicants consistently had an advantage in admission.

The arithmetic is correct, and the vertical axis begins at zero. The limitation is that the chart conceals which departments applicants applied to. Because departments differ substantially in their admission rates, the overall comparison does not reveal what happened within each department.

Figure 2: Admission Rates Within Departments

Contextualized comparison

This paired dot plot separates the admission rates by department while retaining the same underlying application data. Each row represents one department, and the two dots show the admission rates for male and female applicants. The shared horizontal scale makes the differences within each department straightforward to compare.

Female applicants had higher admission rates in departments A, B, D, and F, while male applicants had higher rates in departments C and E. This differs from the overall comparison in Figure 1. The plot reveals that the aggregate rates conceal meaningful differences in admission rates and applicant distributions across departments.

Design Decisions

For Figure 1, I chose a simple bar chart because it makes the overall difference between the two admission rates immediately apparent. I deliberately designated it as misleading because aggregation removes department-level context, not because the values or axis are distorted. This demonstrates that a visualization can use accurate numbers and conventional graphical design yet still encourage an incomplete interpretation when important structure is omitted.

For Figure 2, I used a paired dot plot to separate the comparisons by department. A grouped bar chart was another reasonable option, but the dot plot makes it easy to compare the positions of two admission rates along the same quantitative axis without adding unnecessary filled areas. Keeping the percentage scale identical across departments supports direct comparison. Together, the figures illustrate why the level at which data are summarized is an important visualization decision.

How Visualizations Can Mislead

It can be surprisingly easy to mislead readers without altering a single data point. Choosing which observations to aggregate, which categories to combine, and which comparisons to emphasize can change the story that a chart appears to tell. Figure 1 is an example: its overall admission rates are correct, but the aggregation masks variation among departments. A reader who sees only that figure might incorrectly assume that the aggregate difference describes the pattern within every department.

Readers should therefore look beyond a chart's headline and ask how the data were collected, what the denominator is, which groups have been combined, and whether relevant subgroups show a different pattern. They should also distinguish an observed association from a causal explanation. In this example, the department-level comparisons offer essential context, but neither figure alone establishes whether discrimination occurred or explains every factor affecting admissions. Good visualization should make the relevant structure visible rather than encourage a conclusion that the data cannot support.

Sources and Interpretation

The Berkeley admissions dataset is a classic example used to discuss Simpson's paradox. The figures in this project are based on the admission and rejection counts for departments A through F. Admission rates are calculated as the number admitted divided by the total number of applicants in each group.

These comparisons are descriptive. They illustrate how aggregated and department-specific admission rates differ, but they do not by themselves establish the presence or absence of discrimination or identify a causal mechanism.
